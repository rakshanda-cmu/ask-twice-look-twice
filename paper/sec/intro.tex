\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


Ask a vision-language model a question about an image and you must decide,
often without thinking about it, whether the question goes before the image or
after it. The information content is identical either way. Under causal
attention, putting the question first is the option that lets every image token
see it, which is what a reader would want when told to look for something
specific. It makes the model worse, and on one benchmark category it more than
halves accuracy.

Prior work by the present authors \citep{anon-workshop}, included in the
supplementary material, established this paradox on three yes/no benchmarks and
diagnosed it as a read-out failure. Section~\ref{sec:related} states exactly
what is recapped here and what is new; Sections~\ref{sec:paradox}
and~\ref{sec:anatomy} compress the diagnosis, which the new results need.

Three questions that work could not answer motivate this paper, and each turns
out to have a sharp answer.

\emph{Does the paradox survive outside yes/no visual question answering?} We run
the full ordering ladder on 14 benchmarks not previously tested, including four
video QA datasets and RF20-VL, a $24{,}449$-item detection-style presence
benchmark. Question-first hurts on 12 of 13 image and video benchmarks, costing
$7.0$ points on average and as much as $18.0$. The same breadth exposes two
boundaries the original three benchmarks could not. On video, where the echo
doubles an already long frame sequence, image echoing loses on three of four
datasets. On RF20-VL detection, where the model emits boxes rather than an answer
token, the paradox itself reverses: question-first is $2.80$ mAP \emph{better} and
wins on 17 of 20 datasets, which places the effect in read-out rather than in
prompt order as such.

\emph{Which patches change, and can we find them without cherry-picking?} The
qualitative evidence for steering has so far been chosen by hand, one persuasive
image at a time, which is the kind of evidence a reader should distrust. We
replace it with a procedure: decode every image patch under both orderings with a
logit lens, drop the patches whose top-1 token is unchanged, and rank what
survives by whether the new token belongs to the question's own vocabulary. The
figure reports every patch that changed, and the ranking, not the author, decides
which are worth naming. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} is that output.

\emph{What is the echo actually made of?} If the echo re-anchors read-out rather
than supplying a second look, it should survive being destroyed as an image. It
does. Downscaling the echoed copy to $\tfrac{1}{8}$ resolution, $\tfrac{1}{64}$ of
the pixels, leaves NaturalBench statistically indistinguishable from a
full-resolution echo ($p = 1.00$) while still beating question-last
($p = 0.005$), and cuts the price from $3{,}352$ extra tokens per query to $107$.
RF20 is the lone exception, and an informative one: its targets are small, so the
second look there does real perceptual work.

\emph{Would careful prompt engineering do the same job?} We give GEPA
\citep{gepa2025}, a reflective prompt optimizer, the same budget under both
orderings. It improves question-first on all three benchmarks it is run on and
finds nothing under question-last, which is what the read-out diagnosis predicts,
since only the broken ordering has headroom. It closes at most $83\%$ of the gap
and never reaches the plain question-last baseline.

Together these say something the original result did not: the repair is not about
giving the model a better look at the image but about where the tokens sit, it
costs about a hundred tokens rather than a doubled prompt, and rewording cannot
replace it. We also report where it fails, which is video and any task whose
evidence is small enough that a degraded second look loses it.
