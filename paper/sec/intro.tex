\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


Ask a vision-language model a question about an image and you must decide,
often without thinking about it, whether the question goes before the image or
after it. The information content is identical either way. Under causal
attention, putting the question first is the option that lets every image token
see it, which is what a reader would want when told to look for something
specific. It makes the model worse, and on one benchmark category it more than
halves accuracy.

Prior work by the present authors \citep{anon-workshop}, included in the
supplementary material, established this paradox on three yes/no benchmarks and
diagnosed it as a read-out failure. Section~\ref{sec:related} states exactly
what is recapped here and what is new; Sections~\ref{sec:paradox}
and~\ref{sec:anatomy} compress the diagnosis, which the new results need.

Three questions that work could not answer motivate this paper, and each turns
out to have a sharp answer.

\emph{Does the paradox survive outside yes/no visual question answering?} We run
the full ordering ladder on 14 benchmarks not previously tested, including four
video QA datasets and a $24{,}449$-item detection-style presence benchmark
(RF20-VL). Question-first hurts on 12 of 13 image and video benchmarks, costing
$7.0$ points on average, and on RF20's yes/no presence task it hurts on 7 of 7
categories, taking Lab Imaging from $86.3$ to $38.9$. The same breadth exposes
two boundaries the original three benchmarks could not. On video, where the echo
doubles an already long frame sequence, image echoing loses to question-last on
three of four datasets. On RF20-VL detection, where the model emits boxes rather
than an answer token, the paradox itself reverses: question-first is $2.80$ mAP
\emph{better} and wins on 17 of 20 datasets, which places the effect in
read-out rather than in prompt order as such.

\emph{Which patches change, and can we find them without cherry-picking?} The
qualitative evidence for steering has so far been chosen by hand, one
persuasive image at a time, which is exactly the kind of evidence a reader
should distrust. We replace that with a procedure. For a given example we
decode every image patch under both orderings with a logit lens, drop the
patches whose top-1 token is unchanged, and rank what survives by whether the
new token belongs to the question's own vocabulary. Nothing is selected by eye:
the figure reports every patch that changed, and the ranking, not the author,
decides which ones are worth naming. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} is that output.

\emph{What is the echo actually made of?} If the echo re-anchors read-out rather
than supplying a second look, it should survive being destroyed as an image. It
does. Downscaling the echoed copy to $\tfrac{1}{8}$ resolution, $\tfrac{1}{64}$
of the pixels, leaves NaturalBench group accuracy statistically indistinguishable
from a full-resolution echo ($37.32$ vs $37.37$, $p = 1.00$) while still beating
question-last ($p = 0.005$), and it cuts the price from $3{,}352$ extra tokens
per query to $107$. RF20 is the lone exception, and an informative one: its
targets are small, so there the second look is doing real perceptual work and
degrading it costs $1.76$ F1.

\emph{Would careful prompt engineering do the same job?} We give GEPA
\citep{gepa2025}, a reflective prompt optimizer, the same budget under both
orderings. It improves question-first on all three benchmarks it is run on and
finds nothing at all under question-last, which is what the read-out diagnosis
predicts, since only the broken ordering has headroom to recover. It closes at
most $83\%$ of the gap and never reaches the plain question-last baseline.

Together these say something the original result did not: the repair is not
about giving the model a better look at the image, it is about where the tokens
sit, it costs about a hundred tokens rather than a doubled prompt, and it cannot
be replaced by rewording. We also report where it fails, which is video and any
task whose evidence is small enough that a degraded second look loses it.
