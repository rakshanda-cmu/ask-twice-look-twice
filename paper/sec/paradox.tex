\section{The question-first paradox}
\label{sec:paradox}

\subsection{Moving the question earlier makes the model worse}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} runs the six orderings on four benchmarks with
Qwen3-VL-8B. Every column uses the same system message, the same question
string, the same image, the same greedy decoding and the same scoring code.
The only variable is where the question sits.

Question-first (\sti{}) is the worst ordering on all four benchmarks, and the
margins are not small. NaturalBench group accuracy falls from $35.05$ to
$26.95$, Winoground from $31.75$ to $22.25$, and RF20 F1 from $78.57$ to
$65.59$. On NaturalBench this is $+0.081$ group accuracy for question-last with
a paired bootstrap 95\% CI of $[+0.058, +0.103]$ and McNemar
$p = 1.3\times10^{-12}$; on Winoground it is $+0.095$, CI $[+0.050, +0.140]$,
$p = 7.7\times10^{-5}$.

Intuition predicts the opposite. Placing the question first is what a reader
would do when told to look for something specific, and it is what
retrieval-style prompting recommends. The model sees identical information
either way, and under causal attention the question-first layout gives every
image token access to the question.

\subsection{Question-first perceives better and answers worse}
\label{sec:paradox-steering}

The obvious explanation is that question-first simply degrades the model's
perception of the image, and everything follows from that. It is wrong, and the
evidence against it is the reason this paper exists.

We measure perception directly with a logit lens over image patches. For each
NaturalBench candidate we count how many image patches decode, at their peak
layer, a content word from the question. Across 160 candidates, question-first
makes $6.975$ patches per image decode a question word, against $2.800$ for
question-last, a factor of $2.49$ and a mean gap of $+4.175$ patches. The effect
peaks late: 76 of 160 candidates peak at layer 30 or beyond.
Figure~\ref{fig:steering} shows what this looks like patch by patch. Asking
``Is the adult in the image chasing a child?'' before the image turns
\emph{running} into \emph{chase}, \emph{boy} into \emph{ahead} and
\emph{baseball} into \emph{child}, taking the count of patches decoding a
question word from 1 to 12. The model then answers ``No'' to a question whose
ground truth is Yes, while question-last, whose patches still say
\emph{running} and \emph{boy}, answers ``Yes''.

So the question does reach the image, and it reaches it more strongly in
precisely the ordering that scores worst. The model that looks more like it
found the answer is the model that gets it wrong.

\begin{finding}
\textbf{Finding.} Question-first is the ordering that perceives best and answers
worst. It makes $2.49\times$ more image patches decode a question content word
than question-last ($6.975$ vs $2.800$ per image, $n = 160$), while scoring
$8.1$ points lower on NaturalBench group accuracy
($p = 1.3\times10^{-12}$). Degraded perception cannot explain the deficit.
\end{finding}

\subsection{The paradox is not one model's quirk, but it is not universal either}

Running the same ladder on NaturalBench for five models
(Table~\ref{tab:models-nb} in Appendix~\ref{app:models}) shows four of the five
reproduce the paradox, two of them far more severely than Qwen3-VL-8B.
Qwen2.5-VL-7B loses $17.6$ points of group accuracy when the question moves
first ($27.74 \to 10.16$), and LLaVA-1.5 falls from $12.32$ to exactly $0.00$,
never getting all four items of any group right. InternVL3-8B loses $7.9$.

Gemma-3-27B is the exception and we flag it rather than bury it: its
question-first and question-last scores are within noise
($-0.006$ group accuracy, $p = 0.62$). Section~\ref{sec:crossmodel} shows the
same for Gemma-4-31B. A model can be free of this failure. Most of the ones we
tested are not.

\begin{finding}
\textbf{Finding.} The paradox generalizes across model families without being
universal. Question-first costs Qwen2.5-VL-7B $17.6$ and LLaVA-1.5 $12.3$ points
of NaturalBench group accuracy, with LLaVA-1.5 reaching exactly $0.0$, while
Gemma-3-27B shows no effect ($+0.58$, $p = 0.62$).
\end{finding}
