\section{Anatomy of the Paradox}
\label{sec:anatomy}

Two explanations come to mind, and both are wrong. A length or budget effect is
ruled out immediately: \sti{} and \sit{} hold the same tokens in a different
order, so their prompts are identical in length (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). The
other, that question-first degrades perception, takes the rest of this section
to reject.

\subsection{Perception probe: the question reaches the image, and rewrites it}
\label{sec:anatomy-steering}

Reading image patches with a logit lens, question-first makes $6.975$ patches
per image decode a question content word against $2.800$ for question-last, a
factor of $2.49$ over 160 NaturalBench candidates. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}
shows this patch by patch: asking ``Is the adult in the image chasing a child?''
before the image turns \emph{running} into \emph{chase}, \emph{boy} into
\emph{ahead} and \emph{baseball} into \emph{child}, taking the count of patches
decoding a question word from 1 to 12, and the model then answers ``No'' to a
question whose ground truth is Yes.

The rewrite shows in the representations too: against a forward pass on the
image alone, \sti{}'s layer-18 image tokens sit at mean cosine $0.8627$ where
\sit{}'s sit at $0.9110$ and \ist{}'s are untouched
(Appendix~\ref{app:cosine}). Causal masking supplies a control that matters
later: a question copy placed \emph{after} the image provably cannot alter the
image tokens, and the measurement confirms it,
$\cos(\sti{}, \stit{}) = 0.99999$.


\subsection{Finding the rewrite without cherry-picking}
\label{sec:anatomy-diff}

The evidence above comes from a procedure, not from inspection, because the
alternative is choosing the persuasive image and hoping it is typical. We run
the model under both orderings, project every image patch through the
unembedding, take the top-1 token per patch, and drop the patches whose token is
unchanged. Of those that remain we mark the ones whose new token belongs to the
question's vocabulary, matched by stem, so a patch turning \emph{throws} into
\emph{chase} counts and one turning \emph{lush} into \emph{grass} does not. The
output is a count with a denominator rather than a gallery, it is exhaustive so
a reader can check the changes we did not name, and it costs two forward passes.
We ship it as an interactive viewer in the supplementary material, which steps
through the changes one at a time; the paper figures are frames of it.

\subsection{Read-out probe: the question is under-attended, and late}
\label{sec:anatomy-readout}

If perception is intact, the failure is downstream of it. Probing the answer
position on the $150$ NaturalBench pairs where question-first is wrong and
question-last is right, question-first sends $2.19\times$ less attention to the
question at the peak read-out layer ($0.0676$ vs $0.1479$) and over twice as
much to the image. The timing matters as much as the size: the two orderings are
indistinguishable at chance through layer 25 of 36, then fork at layer 26 and
reach $P(\text{correct})$ of $0.0986$ and $0.8747$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).
A failure that appears only in the last quarter of the network is not an
encoding failure.


\subsection{Causal intervention: each ordering depends on a different path}
\label{sec:anatomy-knockout}

The attention numbers are correlational; severing the edges makes the dependence
causal and doubly dissociated (Appendix~\ref{app:knockout}). Cutting
answer-to-question costs question-last $5.6$ points (95\% CI $[+1.99, +9.60]$)
and question-first nothing ($+0.4$); cutting answer-to-image costs question-first
$9.6$ points and question-last nothing; an equal-length random span moves both by
at most $0.8$. Question-last answers by reading the question. Question-first
answers by reading the image, into which it has already folded the question, and
that path does not carry the answer.
